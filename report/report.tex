\title{Forecasting Lake Michigan--Huron Water Levels\\ One to Six Months Ahead:\\[2pt]
\large Does Machine Learning Add Skill Beyond a Water-Balance Model?}
\author{Samundra Pathak\\ University of Wisconsin--Green Bay}
\date{October 12, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Lake Michigan--Huron rose 1.88\,m between its record low in January 2013 and a near-record
high in July 2020. This study asks whether a machine-learning model can forecast the monthly
mean lake level one to six months ahead better than (1) the assumption that the level does not
change and (2) a simple regression built on the lake's water balance. Seventy-four years of
public NOAA and GLOS data (1950--2023) were merged into one monthly table of level,
over-lake precipitation, evaporation and runoff. Five forecast models were compared in a
rolling-origin backtest over 44 test years (1980--2023): persistence, seasonal climatology, a
19-parameter water-balance regression, gradient-boosted trees, and a hybrid in which the trees
correct the water-balance errors. Every model roughly halves the error of persistence, but most
of that gain comes from the seasonal cycle alone. The water-balance model reached a
root-mean-square error of 3.22\,cm at one month and 14.66\,cm at six months. Gradient boosting
was slightly worse at every horizon (3.32\,cm and 15.39\,cm), with differences inside the
sampling uncertainty, and the hybrid was no better than the water-balance model. When the same
water-balance model was given the precipitation, evaporation and runoff that actually occurred,
its error fell to 2.39\,cm and 8.49\,cm. The limit on forecast skill is therefore the unknown
future weather, not the flexibility of the model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Lakes Michigan and Huron are joined at the Straits of Mackinac and stand at the same elevation,
so hydraulically they are one lake with a surface area of about 117{,}400\,km$^2$. Its level
matters to shoreline property, shipping, harbours and wetlands, and it moves slowly but far:
the record low of January 2013 was followed seven years later by a near-record high.

Forecasting that level looks like a natural machine-learning problem, because long records of
the level and of the water entering and leaving the lake are public. It is also a problem with
well-understood physics: the level changes only because water is added or removed. This study
uses that fact as a test. A model that knows the structure of the water balance is compared
with a flexible machine-learning model that is given the same information and has to find the
structure by itself.

The question is narrow and testable: when forecasting the monthly mean level one to six months
ahead, does gradient boosting beat persistence, and does it beat a physics-based water-balance
regression? The goal is an honest comparison, including the case where machine learning does
not win.

\section{Physical Background}
\label{sec:physics}

\subsection{Conservation of mass for a lake}

The volume of water stored in the lake, $V$, changes only through the fluxes across its
boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}):
\begin{equation}
\frac{dV}{dt} = A\,(p - e) + Q_R + Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}},
\label{eq:volume}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the lake surface area, $p$ and $e$ are the precipitation and evaporation rates
per unit area of lake surface, $Q_R$ is the river runoff from the surrounding land basin,
$Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the inflow from Lake Superior through the St.~Marys River, and
$Q_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the outflow to Lake St.~Clair through the St.~Clair River. Smaller terms
(groundwater exchange, the Chicago diversion, and thermal expansion of the water column) are
neglected.

The shoreline is steep enough that the area barely changes over the observed range of levels
(about 2\,m), so $A$ can be treated as constant and $dV = A\,dL$. Dividing
Equation~\ref{eq:volume} by $A$ turns every term into a depth of water over the lake per unit
time:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dL}{dt} = p - e + \frac{Q_R}{A} + \frac{Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}}}{A}.
\label{eq:level}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Net basin supply}

Integrating Equation~\ref{eq:level} over one month gives the three supply terms as depths in
millimetres: $P_t$, $E_t$ and $R_t$ for month $t$. Their combination is the \emph{net basin
supply},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NBS}_t = P_t - E_t + R_t ,
\label{eq:nbs}
\end{equation}
which is the amount the lake would rise in month $t$ if nothing flowed in or out through the
connecting channels.

Precipitation and evaporation are published as depths over each lake separately. They are
combined as an area-weighted average,
\begin{equation}
P_t = w_M P_t^{\mathrm{Mic}} + w_H P_t^{\mathrm{Hur}}, \qquad
w_M = \frac{A_M}{A_M + A_H}, \quad w_H = 1 - w_M ,
\label{eq:areaweight}
\end{equation}
and likewise for $E_t$, with $A_M = 57{,}800\,\mathrm{km^2}$ and
$A_H = 59{,}600\,\mathrm{km^2}$~\cite{epa}, so $w_M = 0.492$.

Runoff is published as a flow in $\cms$ for each basin. Flows add, so the two basins are summed
and the monthly volume is spread over the combined lake:
\begin{equation}
R_t = \frac{\left(Q_t^{\mathrm{Mic}} + Q_t^{\mathrm{Hur}}\right)\, N_t \times 86{,}400\ \mathrm{s}}
           {A_M + A_H} \times 1000\ \mathrm{mm\,m^{-1}},
\label{eq:runoffdepth}
\end{equation}
where $N_t$ is the number of days in month $t$. As a check on the scale, a combined runoff of
$1500\,\cms$ over a 30-day month is $3.9\times10^{9}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, which is 33\,mm over
$1.174\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Because the lake is so large, a month of supply moves the
level by only a few centimetres.

\subsection{Why the level change spans two months of supply}
\label{sec:twolags}

The level record is a series of monthly \emph{means}, not end-of-month values. Suppose the
level rises at a constant rate $s_t$ (in mm per month) during month $t$, and let $\ell$ be the
level at the boundary between months $t-1$ and $t$. The mean level in month $t$ is then
$\ell + \tfrac12 s_t$, and the mean in month $t-1$ is $\ell - \tfrac12 s_{t-1}$. The change
between the two monthly means is
\begin{equation}
\dL_t \equiv L_t - L_{t-1} = \tfrac12\,s_{t-1} + \tfrac12\,s_t .
\label{eq:twomonth}
\end{equation}
The change in the monthly mean level therefore depends equally on the supply of the current
and the previous month. This is why Figure~\ref{fig:wbcheck} uses a two-month mean of NBS, and
why the water-balance model in Section~\ref{sec:wbmodel} carries two lags of every supply term.

\subsection{Outflow depends on the level}

The channel flows are not in the dataset, but their behaviour is known in outline. The
St.~Clair River is an unregulated channel, so its discharge increases with the level of the
lake that feeds it. Linearising about a reference level $L_{\mathrm{ref}}$,
\begin{equation}
Q_{\mathrm{out}} \approx Q_0 + k\,(L - L_{\mathrm{ref}}), \qquad k > 0 .
\label{eq:stage}
\end{equation}
Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:level}, the channel term becomes a constant net loss plus a
restoring term proportional to the level:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dL}{dt} = \mathrm{NBS} - c - \frac{k}{A}\,(L - L_{\mathrm{ref}}),
\qquad c = \frac{Q_0 - Q_{\mathrm{in}}}{A}.
\label{eq:relax}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:relax} is a first-order relaxation. A high lake drains faster and a low lake
drains more slowly, so the level is pulled back towards an equilibrium with an e-folding time
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{A}{k}.
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
The lake acts as a low-pass filter on the supply: a single wet month has a small effect, while
several wet years in a row can raise the level by more than a metre.

\subsection{The seasonal cycle of supply}

Each supply term has a strong seasonal cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:data}, right). Runoff peaks in
spring, when the winter snowpack melts. Evaporation from a lake follows the bulk aerodynamic
relation
\begin{equation}
e \;\propto\; U\,\bigl[q_s(T_w) - q_a\bigr],
\label{eq:bulk}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the wind speed, $q_s(T_w)$ is the saturation specific humidity at the water
surface temperature $T_w$, and $q_a$ is the specific humidity of the overlying air.
Evaporation is therefore largest not in summer but in late autumn and early winter, when the
water is still warm from summer and cold, dry air moves over it. Equation~\ref{eq:bulk} is
included only to explain the shape of the cycle; the models in this study use the published
evaporation depths and do not compute evaporation themselves.

Together these cycles make the lake rise every spring and fall every autumn. A forecast that
knows only the calendar already captures this, which is why a seasonal baseline is needed in
addition to persistence.

\subsection{Building the monthly table}

The two lakes were combined with Equations~\ref{eq:areaweight} and~\ref{eq:runoffdepth}, and

\subsection{Target definition}

The level this month is almost the same as the level last month, so a model can score well on
the level itself simply by repeating the last value. The target is therefore the \emph{change}
in level. For a forecast issued at the end of month $t$ (the origin) with horizon $h$ months,
\begin{equation}
y_{t,h} = L_{t+h} - L_t , \qquad h = 1,\dots,6 ,
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
and the forecast level is $\hat L_{t+h} = L_t + \hat y_{t,h}$. The forecast may use levels and
$P$, $E$, $R$ up to and including month $t$ and nothing later.

\subsection{Does supply explain level change?}

Before any modelling, Figure~\ref{fig:wbcheck} tests the water-balance picture directly. By
Equation~\ref{eq:twomonth}, the change in the monthly mean level should follow the mean NBS of
the current and previous month with a slope of 1. The observed correlation is 0.81 and the
slope is 1.07. The intercept is $-69$\,mm per month. In Equation~\ref{eq:relax} this is the
constant $c$: the average net loss through the connecting channels, which the dataset does not
include. Spread over the lake area, 69\,mm per month corresponds to a net outflow of roughly
$3{,}100\,\cms$.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Baseline models}

\textbf{Persistence.} The level does not change:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_{t,h} = 0 .
\label{eq:persistence}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Seasonal climatology.} Each future month changes by the long-term average change for
that calendar month. If $\overline{\dL}_m$ is the mean of $\dL$ over all training months that
fall in calendar month $m$, and $m(t)$ is the calendar month of month $t$, then
\begin{equation}
\hat y_{t,h} = \sum_{k=1}^{h} \overline{\dL}_{m(t+k)} .
\label{eq:climatology}
\end{equation}
This baseline was added because a model that only learned the calendar would beat persistence
easily, and that should not be mistaken for forecasting skill.

\subsection{Water-balance model}
\label{sec:wbmodel}

The water-balance model is a linear regression with the structure of
Equations~\ref{eq:twomonth} and~\ref{eq:relax}:
\begin{equation}
\dL_t = a_{m(t)} + \sum_{c \in \{P,E,R\}} \bigl( b_{c,0}\,X_{c,t} + b_{c,1}\,X_{c,t-1} \bigr)
        + g\,\bigl(L_{t-1} - L_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr) + \varepsilon_t ,
\label{eq:wb}
\end{equation}
where $X_{P,t}$, $X_{E,t}$, $X_{R,t}$ are the precipitation, evaporation and runoff depths in
month $t$ and $L_{\mathrm{ref}} = 176.5$\,m. There are 12 monthly intercepts, 6 supply
coefficients and 1 level coefficient, 19 parameters in total, fitted by ordinary least
squares.

Each part has a physical meaning. The two lags per supply term come from the monthly averaging
(Equation~\ref{eq:twomonth}). The monthly intercepts $a_m$ stand in for the mean net channel
loss $c$, which varies through the year. The level coefficient $g$ plays the role of $-k/A$ in
Equation~\ref{eq:relax} and should be negative. A perfect water budget would give
$b_{P,0}+b_{P,1} = 1$, $b_{E,0}+b_{E,1} = -1$ and $b_{R,0}+b_{R,1} = 1$.

\textbf{Forecasting with unknown future supply.} To forecast, future $P$, $E$ and $R$ are
needed but unknown. Each is replaced by its calendar-month average $\overline{X}_{c,m}$ plus a
decaying share of its latest anomaly, a first-order autoregression:
\begin{equation}
\hat X_{c,t+k} = \overline{X}_{c,m(t+k)} + \phi_c^{\,k}\,\bigl(X_{c,t} - \overline{X}_{c,m(t)}\bigr),
\label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_c$ is the lag-one autocorrelation of the anomalies of component $c$ in the
training data. The model is then stepped forward one month at a time,
\begin{equation}
\hat L_{t+k} = \hat L_{t+k-1} + \widehat{\dL}_{t+k}, \qquad \hat L_t = L_t ,
\label{eq:recursion}
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{\dL}_{t+k}$ from Equation~\ref{eq:wb} using the forecast supply and the forecast
level of the previous step.

\textbf{Perfect forcing (diagnostic, not a forecast).} The same model is also run with the
observed $X_{c,t+k}$ in place of Equation~\ref{eq:ar1}. This uses information from the future
and is not a forecast. It measures how much of the error comes from not knowing future weather.

\subsection{Gradient boosting}

The machine-learning model is gradient-boosted regression trees~\cite{friedman}, using
scikit-learn~\cite{sklearn}. A separate model is trained for each horizon $h$ and predicts
$y_{t,h}$ directly from a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_t$. The prediction is a sum of $M$ small
trees,
\begin{equation}
F_M(\mathbf{x}) = F_0 + \nu \sum_{m=1}^{M} T_m(\mathbf{x}),
\label{eq:gb}
\end{equation}
where $F_0$ is the mean of the training targets and each tree $T_m$ is fitted to the residuals
left by the trees before it,
\begin{equation}
r_i^{(m)} = y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i),
\label{eq:gbresid}
\end{equation}
which is the negative gradient of the squared-error loss. The learning rate $\nu$ shrinks the
contribution of each tree. The settings were $M = 300$ trees of depth 3, $\nu = 0.03$, each
tree fitted to a random 80\% of the rows, and at least 5 rows per leaf. They were fixed in
advance and were not tuned on the test years.

The 18 features contain the same information the water-balance model sees, plus longer memory
(Table~\ref{tab:features}). All of them look backward only.

\textbf{Hybrid.} In the hybrid model the trees are trained on the errors of the water-balance
forecast instead of on the level change:
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{\,\mathrm{hyb}}_{t,h} = \hat y^{\,\mathrm{WB}}_{t,h} + F^{(h)}_M(\mathbf{x}_t),
\qquad F^{(h)}_M \ \text{trained on} \ \ y_{t,h} - \hat y^{\,\mathrm{WB}}_{t,h} .
\label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
This is the most direct test of whether machine learning can find structure that the
physics-based model misses.

\subsection{Rolling-origin backtest}

A random train/test split would leak information, because neighbouring months are strongly
correlated and the features use rolling windows. Instead, for each test year $Y$ from 1980 to
2023, every model is refitted on all months before January of $Y$ and then forecasts each month
of $Y$ at each horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:backtest}). The forecast for target month $T$ at
horizon $h$ is issued from origin $T-h$. The first training set covers 1950--1979. This gives
$44 \times 12 = 528$ forecasts per model and horizon, each made by a model that never saw the
year being tested.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

Accuracy is reported as the root-mean-square error of the forecast level, in centimetres, for
each horizon:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}_h = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat L_{i,h} - L_{i}\bigr)^2},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the $n = 528$ target months. The improvement of a model over a
reference model is the relative reduction in RMSE,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Skill}_h = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_h^{\mathrm{model}}}{\mathrm{RMSE}_h^{\mathrm{ref}}} .
\label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}

To judge whether two models really differ, the 44 test years were resampled with replacement
$B = 2000$ times. For each resample $b$ the difference
$d^{(b)}_h = \mathrm{RMSE}^{A,(b)}_h - \mathrm{RMSE}^{B,(b)}_h$ was computed, and the 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles of $d^{(b)}_h$ give a 95\% interval. Whole years are resampled as blocks
because errors within a year, and across overlapping multi-month forecasts, are strongly
correlated.

The importance of a group of features $G$ to the gradient-boosting model is measured by
permutation: the columns in $G$ are shuffled across the held-out rows, which destroys their
information while keeping their distribution, and the increase in error is recorded,
\begin{equation}
I_G = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{RMSE}\bigl(\text{$G$ shuffled, repeat $k$}\bigr)
      - \mathrm{RMSE}\bigl(\text{unshuffled}\bigr), \qquad K = 20 .
\label{eq:perm}
\end{equation}

\textbf{All models beat persistence by a wide margin.} The water-balance model reduces
persistence RMSE by 52\% at one month and 41\% at six months. But seasonal climatology alone
already reduces it by 38--44\%. The seasonal cycle is the largest single source of
predictability.

\textbf{Skill beyond the seasonal cycle fades quickly.} Relative to seasonal climatology, the
water-balance model reduces RMSE by 22\% at one month, 10\% at two months, 7\% at three months,
and about 4\% at four to six months. Its advantage is statistically clear at one to three
months (95\% intervals exclude zero) and not at four to six months.

\textbf{Machine learning did not add skill.} Gradient boosting had higher RMSE than the
water-balance model at every horizon. The gap grows from 0.10\,cm at one month to 0.72\,cm at
six months, and each 95\% interval includes zero (for example $-0.02$ to $+0.23$\,cm at one
month and $-0.16$ to $+1.62$\,cm at six months). The fair reading is ``no better, and possibly
slightly worse''. At five and six months gradient boosting is no better than seasonal
climatology.

\textbf{The hybrid did not help either.} Letting gradient boosting correct the water-balance
errors changed RMSE by between $-0.08$ and $+0.15$\,cm, with every interval spanning zero.
There is no evidence in this dataset of systematic non-linear structure that the linear
water-balance model leaves behind.

\textbf{The ceiling is set by unknown future weather.} With perfect knowledge of future $P$,
$E$ and $R$, the same water-balance model would have reached 2.39\,cm RMSE at one month and
8.49\,cm at six months, which is 42--47\% below seasonal climatology at every horizon. The gap
between this line and the real forecasts in Figure~\ref{fig:rmse} is far larger than any gap
between the models.

The coefficients are physically sensible in sign. Both lags of every component carry weight, as
Equation~\ref{eq:twomonth} predicts. The sums for precipitation ($0.88$) and evaporation
($-0.81$) are reasonably close to the $\pm1$ expected from mass balance.

The level coefficient is negative, consistent with outflow that increases as the lake rises
(Equation~\ref{eq:stage}). Its size, 23\,mm per month for each metre of level, corresponds
through Equation~\ref{eq:tau} to
\begin{equation}
\tau = -\frac{1}{g} = \frac{1000\ \mathrm{mm}}{23\ \mathrm{mm\ per\ month}}
     \approx 43\ \text{months} \approx 3.6\ \text{years},
\label{eq:taufit}
\end{equation}
and to a stage--discharge slope $k = -gA$ of roughly $1{,}000\,\cms$ per metre of level. These
are values implied by the regression, not measurements of the channel. They do show why the
lake carries multi-year memory: an anomaly in the level decays over years, not months.

The runoff coefficient sums to 1.83. Runoff should not raise the lake by more than its own
volume, so runoff is acting as a proxy for something unmeasured. Plausible candidates are
inflow from Lake Superior, which tends to be high in the same wet periods, and runoff from
ungauged areas that the area-ratio method underestimates. The model should therefore be read
as physics-structured, not as a closed water budget.

The supply terms themselves are barely predictable from their own past: $\phi_c$ is 0.02 for
precipitation, 0.26 for evaporation and 0.45 for runoff. In Equation~\ref{eq:ar1} the anomaly
is multiplied by $\phi_c^{\,k}$, so even for runoff only $0.45^3 \approx 9\%$ of an anomaly is
left after three months. This is why forecast skill decays so quickly: after a month or two,
the best available guess for future supply is the seasonal average.

\subsection{What the machine-learning model relies on}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4_feature_importance.png}
\caption{Permutation importance (Equation~\ref{eq:perm}) for the gradient-boosting model,
trained on 1950--2009 and scored on 2010--2023. Each bar shows how much RMSE increases when one
group of inputs is shuffled.}
\label{fig:importance}
\end{figure}

At one month ahead the model uses recent runoff, the season, evaporation, the latest level
change and precipitation, in that order (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). This matches the
physics: by Equation~\ref{eq:twomonth}, last month's supply is still working its way into this
month's mean level. At three and six months the model is dominated by seasonal information. At
six months the dominant group is evaporation and not the calendar month, but evaporation has
such a strong seasonal cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:data}) that the model is most likely using it as
another way of reading the time of year. Permutation importance cannot separate inputs that
carry the same information, so the ranking among seasonal proxies should not be
over-interpreted. The robust finding is that at longer horizons the model has learned the
seasonal cycle and little else.

\subsection{Case study: the 2013 low to the 2020 high}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_case_study_2013_2020.png}
\caption{Left: observed level and six-month-ahead forecasts, 2012--2021. Right: cumulative
anomalies of the supply terms from February 2013 to July 2020 relative to 1950--2012 monthly
averages. Evaporation is plotted with its sign flipped so that upward always means more water
in the lake.}
\label{fig:case}
\end{figure}

Between January 2013 and July 2020 the lake rose 1.88\,m. Over that period, relative to the
1950--2012 averages, over-lake precipitation delivered about 80\,cm of extra water and runoff
about 83\,cm (Figure~\ref{fig:case}, right). Evaporation worked in the opposite direction: it
was above average and removed about 59\,cm more than usual. The rise was therefore driven by a
sustained wet period, not by reduced evaporation. The net supply anomaly of roughly $+1.0$\,m
is smaller than the observed rise, and the remainder is not attributable with this dataset.
Likely contributors are the seasonal rise from January to July (about 27\,cm on average), lower
outflow while the lake was low (Equation~\ref{eq:stage}), and inflow from Lake Superior.

Both the water-balance and gradient-boosting models tracked the rise at a six-month lead with
similar accuracy (RMSE of 15.0 and 14.6\,cm for 2013--2020, against 26.7\,cm for persistence).
Neither anticipated it. Figure~\ref{fig:case} shows both lagging the two steepest climbs, in
2014 and 2019. This is the expected behaviour of any model that fills in future supply with
averages (Equation~\ref{eq:ar1}): it can follow a multi-year rise step by step as the wet
months arrive, but it cannot foresee that the next six months will be wet again.

\section{Discussion}

The central result is that the two approaches reach almost the same accuracy, and the simpler
one is never worse. The reason is visible in the physics. Equation~\ref{eq:level} is linear in
the supply terms, so a linear regression with the right inputs already has the correct
functional form. The trees have to rediscover that form from a few hundred training months and
have nothing extra to gain from non-linearity. The hybrid model confirms this from the other
side: when the trees are asked only for what the water-balance model missed, they find nothing
systematic.

The perfect-forcing diagnostic locates the real limit. Knowing the future supply would cut the
error by 42--47\%, far more than any change of model. Precipitation anomalies have almost no
month-to-month memory ($\phi_P = 0.02$), so no model that looks only at past supply can know
whether the coming months will be wet.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \emph{Level data end in December 2023.} The level file extends to 2025 but contains no
values after 2023, so 2024 could not be tested.
\item \emph{Early evaporation data.} Lake Michigan evaporation for 1941--1944 appears to be
filled with modelled or climatological values. The analysis starts in 1950 to avoid it.
\item \emph{Runoff coverage changes over time.} The gauged fraction of the basin varies
(Michigan 0.78--0.98, Huron 0.74--0.93 during 1950--2023). It is recorded in the processed
table as a data-quality covariate but is not used as a predictor. Coverage falls sharply in
2024 (Huron about 60\% gauged) and runoff is missing after August 2024. This lies outside the
analysis period but matters for any extension.
\item \emph{No connecting-channel flows.} St.~Marys inflow, St.~Clair outflow and the Chicago
diversion are not in the dataset. The model represents them only through monthly constants and
a level term, and the inflated runoff coefficient suggests other terms are absorbing part of
this gap.
\item \emph{Slow drift.} Evaporation averaged 37--40\,mm per month in the 1970s--1990s and
51--52\,mm per month since 2010. A model trained on earlier decades carries a small positive
bias in later test years (1--2\,cm at six months for the real forecasts, 4.8\,cm for the
perfect-forcing diagnostic).
\item \emph{Level resolution.} Levels are reported to the nearest centimetre, which adds a
small amount of rounding noise to monthly changes.
\item \emph{Scope of the machine-learning test.} One algorithm with one fixed, untuned
configuration was tested on a few hundred training months. The result shows that a reasonable
off-the-shelf model does not beat the water-balance model here. It does not show that no
machine-learning approach could.
\item \emph{Data availability lag.} $P$, $E$ and $R$ for month $t$ are assumed to be available
at the end of month $t$. In practice these estimates are published with a delay, so
operational skill would be somewhat lower.
\end{enumerate}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

For monthly Lake Michigan--Huron levels, a 19-parameter water-balance regression is at least as
accurate as gradient boosting at every horizon from one to six months, and a hybrid of the two
is no better than the water-balance model alone. Most of the achievable improvement over
persistence comes from the seasonal cycle. Knowledge of recent supply adds a further 22\% at
one month and little beyond three months. The diagnostic run with observed weather shows that
forecast error could be cut by nearly half if future precipitation, evaporation and runoff were
known.

The practical implication is that the next gain is more likely to come from better inputs than
from a more flexible model: seasonal climate forecasts of precipitation and temperature,
snowpack at the end of winter (which controls spring runoff), lake surface temperature and ice
cover (which control evaporation through Equation~\ref{eq:bulk}), and measured
connecting-channel flows. Machine learning may well earn its place once those inputs are
available, and this backtest provides the baseline against which to test that.

def runoff_to_depth_mm(flow_cms: pd.Series) -> pd.Series:
    """m^3/s over a month -> equivalent depth (mm) spread over the combined lake."""
    seconds_in_month = flow_cms.index.days_in_month * 86_400
    return flow_cms * seconds_in_month / AREA_COMBINED_M2 * 1000.0


def build_monthly() -> pd.DataFrame:
    w_mic = AREA_MIC_KM2 / (AREA_MIC_KM2 + AREA_HUR_KM2)
    w_hur = 1.0 - w_mic

    # Precip and evap are depths over each lake -> area-weighted average.
    precip = w_mic * read_depth_table("prc_mic_lake.csv") + w_hur * read_depth_table(
        "prc_hur_lake.csv"
    )
    evap = w_mic * read_depth_table("evaporation_mic.csv") + w_hur * read_depth_table(
        "evaporation_hur.csv"
    )

    # Runoff is a volume flux -> add the two basins, then convert to depth.
    ro_mic = read_runoff("runoff_mic_arm.csv")
    ro_hur = read_runoff("runoff_hur_arm.csv")
    runoff = runoff_to_depth_mm(ro_mic["flow_cms"] + ro_hur["flow_cms"])

    df = pd.DataFrame(
        {
            "level_m": read_levels(),
            "precip_mm": precip,
            "evap_mm": evap,
            "runoff_mm": runoff,
            "frac_gauged_mic": ro_mic["fraction_gauged"],
            "frac_gauged_hur": ro_hur["fraction_gauged"],
        }
    )
    df["nbs_mm"] = df["precip_mm"] - df["evap_mm"] + df["runoff_mm"]
    # Target: change in the monthly mean level from the previous month, in mm.
    df["dlevel_mm"] = df["level_m"].diff() * 1000.0

    df = df.loc[START:]
    # Keep only the span where every series is observed.
    complete = df[["level_m", "precip_mm", "evap_mm", "runoff_mm"]].notna().all(axis=1)
    df = df.loc[: complete[complete].index.max()]
    assert df.notna().all().all(), "unexpected gap inside the analysis period"
    df.index.name = "month"
    return df

class Persistence:
    """The level does not change."""

    def fit(self, train):
        return self

    def predict(self, df, origins, horizons):
        return np.zeros((len(origins), len(horizons)))


class SeasonalClimatology:
    """Each future month changes by its long-term average for that calendar month."""

    def fit(self, train):
        self.mean_change_ = train["dlevel_mm"].groupby(month_of(train.index)).mean()
        return self

    def predict(self, df, origins, horizons):
        out = np.zeros((len(origins), len(horizons)))
        for i, origin in enumerate(origins):
            steps = [self.mean_change_[(origin + k).month] for k in range(1, max(horizons) + 1)]
            cum = np.cumsum(steps)
            out[i] = [cum[h - 1] for h in horizons]
        return out

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Water-balance model (Equations~\ref{eq:wb}--\ref{eq:recursion}).}]
COMPONENTS = ["precip_mm", "evap_mm", "runoff_mm"]
LEVEL_REF_M = 176.5  # only centres the level term; has no effect on forecasts


class WaterBalance:
    """Physics-structured regression, stepped forward one month at a time.

        dL[t] = a[month] + sum_c (b0_c * X_c[t] + b1_c * X_c[t-1]) + g * (L[t-1] - ref)

    for c in precipitation, evaporation, runoff.
    - Two lags per component because levels are monthly *means*: the change
      between two monthly means straddles two months of supply.
    - a[month] + g * level stands in for the connecting-channel flows (St. Marys
      in, St. Clair out), which we have no data for; outflow rises with level.

    To forecast, future P/E/R are unknown, so each is replaced by its
    calendar-month climatology plus a decaying share of the latest anomaly
    (AR(1)). With perfect_forcing=True the observed future P/E/R are used
    instead: not a real forecast, but an upper bound on what this model could
    do with a perfect weather forecast.
    """

    def __init__(self, perfect_forcing=False):
        self.perfect_forcing = perfect_forcing

    def _design(self, month, x_now, x_prev, level_prev):
        dummies = np.zeros((len(month), 12))
        dummies[np.arange(len(month)), month - 1] = 1.0
        return np.column_stack([dummies, x_now, x_prev, level_prev - LEVEL_REF_M])

    def fit(self, train):
        x = train[COMPONENTS].to_numpy()
        month = month_of(train.index)
        design = self._design(month[1:], x[1:], x[:-1], train["level_m"].to_numpy()[:-1])
        target = train["dlevel_mm"].to_numpy()[1:]
        self.coef_, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(design, target, rcond=None)

        # Forcing model: monthly climatology + AR(1) on the anomalies.
        self.clim_ = train[COMPONENTS].groupby(month).mean()
        anom = x - self.clim_.loc[month].to_numpy()
        self.phi_ = np.array(
            [np.corrcoef(anom[1:, j], anom[:-1, j])[0, 1] for j in range(len(COMPONENTS))]
        )
        return self

    def predict(self, df, origins, horizons):
        x_all = df[COMPONENTS]
        out = np.zeros((len(origins), len(horizons)))
        for i, origin in enumerate(origins):
            x_prev = x_all.loc[origin].to_numpy()
            anom = x_prev - self.clim_.loc[origin.month].to_numpy()
            level_start = df.at[origin, "level_m"]
            cum = 0.0
            path = {}
            for k in range(1, max(horizons) + 1):
                target = origin + k
                if self.perfect_forcing:
                    x_now = x_all.loc[target].to_numpy()
                else:
                    x_now = self.clim_.loc[target.month].to_numpy() + self.phi_**k * anom
                row = self._design(
                    np.array([target.month]), x_now[None], x_prev[None],
                    np.array([level_start + cum / 1000.0]),
                )
                cum += float(row @ self.coef_)
                path[k] = cum
                x_prev = x_now
            out[i] = [path[h] for h in horizons]
        return out

    def coefficients(self) -> pd.Series:
        names = [f"month_{m}" for m in range(1, 13)]
        names += [f"{c}_lag0" for c in COMPONENTS] + [f"{c}_lag1" for c in COMPONENTS]
        names += ["level_m"]
        return pd.Series(self.coef_, index=names)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Features and gradient boosting, including the hybrid (Equation~\ref{eq:hybrid}).}]
def make_features(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Predictors available at the end of each month (row t uses data up to t only)."""
    f = pd.DataFrame(index=df.index)
    f["level"] = df["level_m"] - LEVEL_REF_M
    for lag in range(3):
        f[f"dlevel_lag{lag}"] = df["dlevel_mm"].shift(lag)
        for c in COMPONENTS:
            f[f"{c[:-3]}_lag{lag}"] = df[c].shift(lag)
    for window in (3, 6, 12):
        f[f"nbs_sum{window}"] = df["nbs_mm"].rolling(window).sum()
    angle = 2 * np.pi * month_of(df.index) / 12
    f["month_sin"] = np.sin(angle)
    f["month_cos"] = np.cos(angle)
    return f


def _future_change(df, h):
    """L[t+h] - L[t] in mm, aligned on t (NaN where t+h is past the end)."""
    return (df["level_m"].shift(-h) - df["level_m"]) * 1000.0


class GradientBoosting:
    """Direct ML forecast: one gradient-boosted tree model per horizon.

    Hyperparameters were fixed in advance (small, slow-learning, shallow trees
    for a few hundred training rows) and were not tuned on the test years.
    If `base` is given, the trees learn the *residual* of that model's forecast
    instead of the level change itself (a physics + ML hybrid).
    """

    params = dict(n_estimators=300, learning_rate=0.03, max_depth=3,
                  subsample=0.8, min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=0)

    def __init__(self, base=None):
        self.base = base

    def fit(self, train):
        self.models_ = {}
        feats = make_features(train)
        ok = feats.notna().all(axis=1)
        if self.base is not None:
            self.base.fit(train)
        for h in range(1, 7):
            y = _future_change(train, h)
            rows = ok & y.notna()
            origins = train.index[rows]
            target = y[rows].to_numpy()
            if self.base is not None:
                target = target - self.base.predict(train, origins, [h])[:, 0]
            self.models_[h] = GradientBoostingRegressor(**self.params).fit(
                feats.loc[origins], target
            )
        return self

    def predict(self, df, origins, horizons):
        # Features are backward-looking, so computing them on the full table
        # does not leak future information into a given origin's row.
        feats = make_features(df).loc[origins]
        out = np.column_stack([self.models_[h].predict(feats) for h in horizons])
        if self.base is not None:
            out = out + self.base.predict(df, origins, horizons)
        return out
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Rolling-origin backtest, RMSE and the year-block bootstrap.}]
def backtest(df: pd.DataFrame, first_test_year=FIRST_TEST_YEAR, verbose=True) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Return one row per (model, target month, horizon) with observed and predicted level."""
    rows = []
    for year in range(first_test_year, df.index.max().year + 1):
        train = df.loc[: f"{year - 1}-12"]
        targets = df.loc[f"{year}-01":f"{year}-12"].index
        models = {name: m.fit(train) for name, m in all_models().items()}
        for h in HORIZONS:
            origins = targets - h
            start = df.loc[origins, "level_m"].to_numpy()
            observed = df.loc[targets, "level_m"].to_numpy()
            for name, model in models.items():
                change_mm = model.predict(df, origins, [h])[:, 0]
                rows.append(pd.DataFrame({
                    "model": name, "target": targets, "origin": origins, "horizon": h,
                    "observed_m": observed, "predicted_m": start + change_mm / 1000.0,
                }))
        if verbose:
            print(f"  tested {year}", end="\r")
    preds = pd.concat(rows, ignore_index=True)
    preds["error_cm"] = (preds["predicted_m"] - preds["observed_m"]) * 100.0
    return preds


def rmse_table(preds: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """RMSE (cm) with models as rows and horizons as columns."""
    rmse = preds.groupby(["model", "horizon"], sort=False)["error_cm"].apply(
        lambda e: np.sqrt(np.mean(e**2))
    )
    return rmse.unstack("horizon")


def bootstrap_rmse_diff(preds, model_a, model_b, n_boot=2000, seed=0) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """RMSE(a) - RMSE(b) per horizon with a 95% interval.

    Whole test years are resampled together because errors within a year (and
    across overlapping multi-month forecasts) are strongly correlated.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    out = {}
    for h in HORIZONS:
        sq = {}
        for name in (model_a, model_b):
            p = preds[(preds["model"] == name) & (preds["horizon"] == h)]
            sq[name] = (p["error_cm"] ** 2).groupby(p["target"].dt.year).agg(["sum", "count"])
        years = sq[model_a].index.to_numpy()
        a, b = sq[model_a].to_numpy(), sq[model_b].to_numpy()

        def diff(idx):
            return (np.sqrt(a[idx, 0].sum() / a[idx, 1].sum())
                    - np.sqrt(b[idx, 0].sum() / b[idx, 1].sum()))

        boots = [diff(rng.integers(0, len(years), len(years))) for _ in range(n_boot)]
        lo, hi = np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])
        out[h] = {"diff_cm": diff(np.arange(len(years))), "ci_low": lo, "ci_high": hi}
    return pd.DataFrame(out).T.rename_axis("horizon")
